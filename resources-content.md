**H1:** How to Start Road Racing: A Guide for Women
**Byline:** By Amber Pate, UCI Women's WorldTour cyclist and coach

**Intro**
If you're riding strongly on group rides and you've wondered, "Could I race?", the answer is almost certainly yes. What usually holds women back isn't fitness. It's not knowing where to start, worrying about crashing or getting dropped, and turning up to a start line where you don't know anyone.

You're not alone in that. Women still make up only around one in five Cycling Australia members, and national research found that more than half of women riders wanted more confidence on the road, while many asked specifically for racing-skills training. The good news is that confidence is a skill you can build, and this guide walks you through how.

I was nervous before my first race too. What I wish I'd known: everyone on the start line had a first race once, and racing skills can be learned.

**H2: Step 1: Get a licence and find a club**
To race in Australia you need AusCycling cover. You have a few options:
- **Try before you commit.** If you've never been an AusCycling member, the 30-day free trial lets you take part in club rides and club-level races at affiliated clubs.
- **Race once or twice.** A one-day or seven-day event licence covers individual club-level events.
- **Race regularly.** Club or Race membership covers you all year, including training.

Then find a local club. Clubs run regular racing, often have women's or graded fields, and are the best place to meet other riders who were in your shoes not long ago.

**H2: Step 2: Choose a beginner-friendly first race**
Criteriums ("crits") are a great place to start. They're short races on a closed loop, so there's no traffic, you're never far from the finish, and if you get dropped you can often keep riding and learning. Club races are usually graded by ability, so you'll be racing riders at a similar level.

Look for:
- Women's or lower-grade fields
- Shorter races (30 to 45 minutes)
- Club events with a relaxed, social feel

**H2: Step 3: Build your bunch skills before race day**
Racing in a bunch is different from a social ride. Riders are closer together, speeds change quickly and you'll corner as a group. Practise these first:
- **Holding a wheel:** riding smoothly 30 to 50 cm behind another rider, eyes looking through them, not at their back wheel.
- **Cornering:** braking before the corner, looking where you want to go, and holding your line.
- **Riding close:** getting comfortable with riders beside you and the occasional bump of shoulders.

My top tip: relax your upper body. Soft elbows, loose grip, eyes up the road. Relaxed riders stay smooth in the bunch.

**H2: Step 4: Train for racing, not just fitness**
Racing isn't steady. It's a series of surges, sprints out of corners and short hard efforts, followed by recovery in the bunch. Add one or two sessions a week of short, hard intervals (for example, 30 to 60 seconds hard with recovery between) to prepare your body for those changes of pace. You don't need more hours; you need the right kind.

**H2: Step 5: Your race-day checklist**
- Arrive early to register, pin your number and ride a lap of the course.
- Warm up properly, including a few short efforts.
- Start near the back. It's calmer, and you can watch and learn.
- Set a learning goal, not a result goal: "stay in the bunch for 15 minutes" or "ride every corner smoothly".
- Talk to other women at the start line. Most remember their first race and are happy to help.

**H2: Frequently asked questions**
**Do I need a race bike?** No. A well-maintained road bike is fine. Check it's in good working order, especially brakes and tyres.

**How fit do I need to be?** If you can comfortably hold the pace on a solid group ride, you're ready to try a graded club race.

**What if I get dropped?** Everyone gets dropped at some point, including professionals. In many crits you can keep riding, practise your skills and learn from it. Getting dropped in your first race is part of the process, not a failure.

**Title:** How Much Should Junior Cyclists Train? A Parent's Guide
**Meta description:** How many hours should a junior cyclist train? Coach and WorldTour rider Amber Pate explains age-appropriate training, recovery and overtraining signs.
**URL slug:** /resources/how-much-should-junior-cyclists-train.html
**H1:** How Much Should Junior Cyclists Train? A Parent's Guide
**Byline:** By Amber Pate, UCI Women's WorldTour cyclist and coach

**Intro**
When your child loves cycling, it's tempting to think more training means more success. They follow pro riders online, join adult bunch rides and want to do everything the older riders do. But for young riders, more is not always better, and too much too soon is one of the most common reasons talented juniors get injured, burn out or leave the sport.

I see too many juniors overtraining too young, often on programs built for adults, not for them.

**H2: The short answer: how many hours?**
Australia's sports medicine bodies have published clear guidance. The Sport Specialisation in Young Athletes position statement (Australian Institute of Sport, Australian Medical Association, Australasian College of Sport and Exercise Physicians and Sports Medicine Australia) recommends:
- **No more than 16 hours a week** of total sport, including training and competition, across all sports.
- **No more hours per week than the athlete's age.** A 14-year-old, for example, should do no more than 14 hours a week.
- **Organised sport should not exceed free play by more than 2 to 1.**

Those are upper limits, not targets. Many developing riders progress well on far less.

**H2: Why more isn't better**
In October 2026 the Australian Sports Commission warned against early specialisation in young athletes, noting that the strongest evidence links it to overuse injuries and burnout, especially when training volumes are high and recovery is inadequate. The AIS position statement also links early specialisation to less enjoyment of sport and earlier drop-out.

Young riders are still growing. Their bodies adapt differently from adults', and they're balancing school, friendships and sleep. A program designed for an elite adult doesn't account for any of that.

**H2: Warning signs of overtraining**
Watch for:
- Ongoing tiredness that doesn't improve with a few easy days
- Results or training getting worse despite training more
- Mood changes, irritability or low motivation
- Poor sleep
- Getting sick more often
- Recurring aches, pains or niggles
- Losing enjoyment of riding

If you notice several of these, reduce training and talk to your GP or a sports doctor. Persistent pain should always be checked by a professional.

**H2: What good junior training looks like**
- **Individualised:** built around the rider's age, maturity, goals and school commitments, not copied from someone else.
- **Varied:** skills sessions, fun rides and other sports, not just hard efforts.
- **Recovery built in:** easy days and regular breaks are part of the plan.
- **Sleep:** the Sleep Health Foundation recommends that teenagers get 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night.
- **Fuel for growth:** young riders need to eat enough to support both growing and training. A sports dietitian can help if you're unsure.
- **Skills and racecraft:** cornering, bunch riding and tactics pay off for years.

Junior riders also race under AusCycling's equipment rules, including gearing limits for younger age groups, so check the current technical regulations for your child's category.

**H2: Questions to ask a coach**
- How will you adjust training around school, exams and growth?
- How do you monitor fatigue and recovery?
- How many hours a week will my child train, and why?
- How do you keep cycling fun?

Every junior plan I write starts with the rider: their age, school and recovery. Skills and enjoyment come first, and training stays within AIS guidance.

**H2: Frequently asked questions**
**Should juniors use power meters?** Data can be useful for older juniors, but it shouldn't replace learning to ride by feel, and young riders shouldn't be chasing numbers every session.

**Should juniors play other sports?** Yes. The AIS recommends delaying specialisation, and variety builds all-round athleticism and reduces overuse injury risk.

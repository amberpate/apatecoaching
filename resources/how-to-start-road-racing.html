How to Start Road Racing: A Guide for Women

By Amber Pate, UCI Women's WorldTour cyclist and coach

If you're riding strongly on group rides and you've wondered, "Could I race?", the answer is almost certainly yes. What usually holds women back isn't fitness. It's not knowing where to start, worrying about crashing or getting dropped, and turning up to a start line where you don't know anyone.

You're not alone in that. Women still make up only around one in five Cycling Australia members, and national research found that more than half of women riders wanted more confidence on the road, while many asked specifically for racing-skills training. The good news is that confidence is a skill you can build, and this guide walks you through how.

I was nervous before my first race too. What I wish I'd known: everyone on the start line had a first race once, and racing skills can be learned.

Step 1: Get a licence and find a club

To race in Australia you need AusCycling cover. You have a few options:

  • Try before you commit. If you've never been an AusCycling member, the 30-day free trial lets you take part in club rides and club-level races at affiliated clubs.
  • Race once or twice. A one-day or seven-day event licence covers individual club-level events.
  • Race regularly. Club or Race membership covers you all year, including training.

Then find a local club. Clubs run regular racing, often have women's or graded fields, and are the best place to meet other riders who were in your shoes not long ago.

Step 2: Choose a beginner-friendly first race

Criteriums ("crits") are a great place to start. They're short races on a closed loop, so there's no traffic, you're never far from the finish, and if you get dropped you can often keep riding and learning. Club races are usually graded by ability, so you'll be racing riders at a similar level.

Look for:

  • Women's or lower-grade fields
  • Shorter races (30 to 45 minutes)
  • Club events with a relaxed, social feel

Step 3: Build your bunch skills before race day

Racing in a bunch is different from a social ride. Riders are closer together, speeds change quickly and you'll corner as a group. Practise these first:

  • Holding a wheel: riding smoothly 30 to 50 cm behind another rider, eyes looking through them, not at their back wheel.
  • Cornering: braking before the corner, looking where you want to go, and holding your line.
  • Riding close: getting comfortable with riders beside you and the occasional bump of shoulders.

My top tip: relax your upper body. Soft elbows, loose grip, eyes up the road. Relaxed riders stay smooth in the bunch.

Step 4: Train for racing, not just fitness

Racing isn't steady. It's a series of surges, sprints out of corners and short hard efforts, followed by recovery in the bunch. Add one or two sessions a week of short, hard intervals (for example, 30 to 60 seconds hard with recovery between) to prepare your body for those changes of pace. You don't need more hours; you need the right kind.

Step 5: Your race-day checklist

  • Arrive early to register, pin your number and ride a lap of the course.
  • Warm up properly, including a few short efforts.
  • Start near the back. It's calmer, and you can watch and learn.
  • Set a learning goal, not a result goal: "stay in the bunch for 15 minutes" or "ride every corner smoothly".
  • Talk to other women at the start line. Most remember their first race and are happy to help.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a race bike? No. A well-maintained road bike is fine. Check it's in good working order, especially brakes and tyres.

How fit do I need to be? If you can comfortably hold the pace on a solid group ride, you're ready to try a graded club race.

What if I get dropped? Everyone gets dropped at some point, including professionals. In many crits you can keep riding, practise your skills and learn from it. Getting dropped in your first race is part of the process, not a failure.
